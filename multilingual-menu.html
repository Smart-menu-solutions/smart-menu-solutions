Multilingual menus: how to reach international guests

Why a menu in several languages pays off, which languages make sense and how a digital QR menu handles it without extra work.

Guides · 25 September 2026 · about 5 minutes to read

A guest who can't read the menu orders cautiously: the dish they already know, or whatever the next table is pointing at. Staff spend time translating, and the house specialities get overlooked. In holiday destinations, big cities and hotels this happens every day. A menu in the guest's own language solves it simply.

Why a multilingual menu pays off

  • Guests order with confidence: when they understand what they'll get, they are more willing to try something new, including your specialities.
  • Fewer questions: staff don't have to explain every dish and have more time for service.
  • A warm welcome: a menu in their own language shows international guests they are welcome.
  • Fewer misunderstandings: ingredients and preparation are described clearly instead of being translated on the spot.

The problem with printed menus in several languages

On paper, a multilingual menu quickly gets cluttered: either several languages are stacked on each page and the menu gets thick, or there is a separate menu per language that has to be reprinted with every price change. In practice, the foreign-language menus are therefore often out of date.

How it works with a digital QR menu

A digital menu can exist in every language you need, with just one QR code on the table. The guest scans the code and switches to their language with one tap at the top of the menu. When you change a price or a dish, it is up to date in every language at once. Nothing needs reprinting.

Which languages make sense?

That depends on your guests. A few pointers:

  • English almost always makes sense, because many international guests understand it.
  • The local language, of course, for example German in Germany and Austria or Greek in Greece.
  • Your most common guests' languages: look at where your guests come from. In holiday destinations that is often German, Italian, French or Spanish.

Tips for good translations

  • Keep original names: a moussaka stays a moussaka. Translate the description underneath instead.
  • Describe briefly and clearly: the main ingredients are enough; flowery wording often gets lost in translation.
  • Stay consistent: translate the same term the same way throughout, for example always "grilled" rather than sometimes "grilled" and sometimes "barbecued".
  • Have it proofread: if possible, let a native speaker take a quick look.
